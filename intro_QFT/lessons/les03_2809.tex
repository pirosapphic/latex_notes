\section{Symmetries and Group Theory}
The principle of symmetry (invariance) is the guiding light to create a theory:
the first and fundamental symmetry is \textit{Lorentz invariance}, because special
relativity states that \textbf{all physical laws must be the same in every inertial
reference frame}; each particle (state) must also observe other symmetries, but those
vary case by case.

The language of symmetries is \textit{group theory}, so, in order to proceed, an
introduction to groups is needed.

\textbf{Def.} A group is a set $G$ with a binary operation $\cdot$ so that
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\forall g_1,g_2\in G$, $g_1\cdot g_2 \in G$;
    \item $\exists e\in G$ so that $\forall g\in G$, $g\cdot e = e \cdot g = g$;
    \item $\forall g\in G$, $\exists g^{-1}$, so that $g^{-1}\cdot g=g\cdot g^{-1}=e$;
    \item $\forall g_1,g_2,g_3\in G$, $g_1 \cdot (g_2 \cdot g_3) = (g_1\cdot g_2)\cdot
	g_3 = g_1\cdot g_2\cdot g_3$.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Def.} A group is \textit{Abelian} if and only if
$$\forall g_1,g_2\in G\text{, }g_1\cdot g_2 = g_2\cdot g_1$$
\subsection{Zoology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item $U$ meaning "unitary" means $UU^\dagger=\mathbb{I}$, for complex valued
	matrices;
    \item $O$ meaning "orthogonal" means $OO^T=\mathbb{I}$, for real valued matrices
    \item $S$ meaning "special" means $\det(S)=1$.
\end{itemize}
\newpage
\textbf{Examples}\\
\begin{itemize}
    \item $U(1)$ is the group of phase rotations in $\mathbb{C}$;
    \item $SU(2)$ is the group of spin;
    \item $SO(3)$ is the group of proper rotations
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Note} The number in the name of the group is the dimensionality of the
fundamental representation.

\section{Lie Groups}
The most important groups are those that represent \textit{continuous transformations},
i.e. every element of the group corresponds to a transformation that can be broken
down into \textit{infinitesimal transformations}: using parameters $\alpha$ and
generators $T$, the element $g(\alpha)\in G$ is
$$g(\alpha) = \mathbb{I}+i\alpha_a T^a + O(\alpha^2) = ... = \exp\left[i\alpha_a T^a
\right]$$
so its generators can be found as
$$T^a = -i\eval{\dv[]{g}{\alpha_a}}_{\alpha=0}$$
it is useful to work with generators as their number is small and they are operators
(that can be represented as matrices).

The operation of the groups can be used to construct the operation between the
generators, by the BCH formula:
$$g_1\cdot g_2 \rightarrow e^X\cdot e^Y := \exp\left(X+Y+\frac{1}{2}[X,Y]+
\frac{1}{12}[X,[X,Y]]\right)$$
where $[\cdot,\cdot]$ is the \textit{Lie commutator}.

\textbf{Def.} The vector space of the generators equipped with a commutator
(bilinear, anti-commutative, Jacobi Identity)
is called a \textbf{Lie Algebra} $\mathfrak{g}$.

\textbf{Note} The most general form of a commutator is 
$$[T^a,T^b] = i f\indices{^a_b_c}T^c$$

\subsection{Example: algebra of SO(3)}
The rotation matrix about the X axis of an angle $\vartheta_1$ is
$$R_1(\vartheta_1)=\begin{pmatrix}
    1&0&0\\
    0&\cos(\vartheta_1)&-\sin(\vartheta_1)\\
    0&\sin(\vartheta_1)&\cos(\vartheta_1)
\end{pmatrix}$$
the first generator is then
$$J_1 = \eval{-i\dv[]{R_1}{\vartheta_1}}_{\vartheta_1=0} = \begin{pmatrix}
    0&0&0\\
    0&0&-i\\
    0&i&0
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The other 2 matrices can be obtained similarly:
$$J_2 = 
\begin{pmatrix}
0&0&i\\
0&0&0\\
-i&0&0
\end{pmatrix}\qquad J_3=
\begin{pmatrix}
0&-i&0\\
i&0&0\\
0&0&0
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The commutation relations turn out to be
$$[J^i,J^j] = i\varepsilon_{ijk}J^k.$$
From the generators, the $J^\pm$ operators are constructed.

\subsection{Example: algebra of SU(2)}
The definition states $SU(2) = \{\text{2x2 unitary matrices with}\det(U)=1\}$: the
conditions imply, by the BCH formula:
\begin{gather*}
    U^\dagger U  =\mathbb{I}\rightarrow J_i^\dagger=J_i\\
    \det(U)=\det(e^{J_i})=1=\exp[\Tr(J_i)] \rightarrow \Tr(J_i)=0
\end{gather*}
the traceless, hermitian matrix vector space has base $\sigma^ii$, the Pauli matrices,
which have commutation relations
$$[\sigma^i,\sigma^j] = i\varepsilon_{ijk}\sigma^k$$
\section{Group representations}
\textbf{Def.} A representation of a group $G$ is a map 
\begin{align*}
&r: G \rightarrow GL(V)\\
&r: g \rightarrow \text{square matrix}
\end{align*}
so that
$$r(g_1\cdot g_2) = r(g_1)\cdot	r(g_2)$$
where the second $\cdot$ is the matrix multiplication.\\
$\dim(V) = n$ is the dimension of the representation.

\textbf{Def.} An irreducible representation is a representation in which no subset
of $\Im(r)$ is a subgroup of $\Im(r)$.

\textbf{Note} A reducible representation can always be block-diagonalized (?).
